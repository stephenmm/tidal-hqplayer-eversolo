Both open questions are now settled

The analogue output cannot carry the beam. The XVF3800’s I2S signal assignment gives the host stream and the DAC stream separate pins — in the USB Accessory configuration I2S_DATA0 is output to DAC while the host stream goes over USB — and the datasheet is explicit that “the I2S interface operates in master mode to supply the far end audio signal to the DAC driving the speaker.” The DAC’s source is the AEC reference by construction, which is what keeps the signal playing into the room identical to the one the echo canceller is subtracting. The AUDIO_MGR_OP_L / AUDIO_MGR_OP_R mux is host-bound: it sets what the XVF3800 sends to the host. Enumerating the whole control-command set turns up no DAC source selector at all. Same architecture across the USB, I2S and Home Assistant firmwares — only the pin changes.

Latency is published, and it is 58 ms. XMOS’s pipeline parameter table gives an input delay of 58 ms minimum, microphone in to I2S out, with a 16 ms beamformer update interval and a 192 ms AEC tail. That is the internal figure; USB transport sits on top. The table’s separate 50 ms “output delay” is the far-end direction and does not add. Quoted as a minimum with no typical or maximum, which reads as a floor rather than a target.

Two details behind those numbers. The 58 ms is XMOS’s published minimum input delay, mic in to I2S out — 928 samples at 16 kHz — and USB transport sits on top of it rather than inside it. The raw microphone channels in the 6-channel firmware are not a shortcut past it: XMOS describes that category as “amplified microphone data with system delay”, time-aligned to the processed output, so they arrive delayed too. For offline work that does not matter at all; for anything live it does.

The separate 50 ms figure in XMOS’s table is the output delay and applies only “if far end processing on device is implemented”. AUDIO_MGR_FAR_END_DSP_ENABLE defaults to 0, so by default the playback path is a pass-through and that 50 ms does not apply — which is why the playback direction is quick.

On levels, since the point of the jack was to get line level out of it: the TLV320AIC3104’s headphone drivers are 30 mW into 16 Ω, about 0.69 Vrms, with separate differential line outputs specified into 10 kΩ. That is a perfectly usable consumer line level — −10 dBV nominal is 0.316 Vrms — so had it carried the beam it would have been genuinely useful. It carries the far end.

The question that decides it: what is actually driving “minimum delay”? That was two words in the brief, interpreted aggressively here — and everything expensive in this design descends from it. If it feeds loudspeakers in the same room, the reSpeaker is out twice over: 58 ms is forty times this budget and far past any Haas allowance, and its analogue output cannot carry the beam anyway. If it feeds a recorder, a codec or an ASR engine, 58 ms is invisible, the beam arriving over USB is exactly the right delivery, and building this is hard to justify on 3.8 dBA alone.

There is no middle case left to measure. If you want the two-minute confirmation anyway: power the board from a USB charger with no host attached, so nothing can be streaming to it, put headphones in the 3.5 mm jack and talk. Silence is the documented behaviour.

Either way, buy one first. Its 6-channel USB firmware exposes all four raw microphone channels alongside the processed output, which makes it a ready-made algorithm sandbox for phase 1 — run this project’s SRP-PHAT and tracker against real recordings from real hardware, for a fraction of what that phase costs otherwise. It will not validate the 513.6 mm aperture. It will tell you whether the tracking behaves, and give you a commercial baseline to beat.

§1

The brief

Three requirements, each of which turns out to force an architectural decision rather than a parameter choice.

“Finds the primary talker”

Fully automatic — no operator, no presets. The system localises, decides who is primary, steers, and hands over cleanly when someone else takes the floor. Deciding is the hard half: per-frame localisation is not talker tracking.

“Minimum delay”

Latency is a first-class constraint, budgeted term by term and traded against directivity explicitly — not whatever falls out of a convenient DSP structure. This rules out the obvious architecture. The textbook broadband beamformer runs in the STFT domain, and a 512-point frame at 16 kHz costs 32 ms of algorithmic latency before any filtering. That is 23× this entire signal chain, so the beamformer here is time-domain FIR throughout.

It also rules out continuous steering. Re-deriving filter coefficients every frame makes latency a function of the tracker. Instead a fixed bank of 34 beams is pre-computed, two run in parallel, and steering is a cross-fade between them — which adds exactly zero delay.

“Line level for output”

Calibrated, standards-compliant analogue output: balanced +4 dBu with real headroom, plus unbalanced −10 dBV. Gain staging is worked end to end, from sound pressure at the diaphragm to volts at the connector.

§2

What a line array cannot do

Stated up front, because it constrains where the product can be mounted.

  • Cone ambiguity. A linear array measures only the angle to its own axis. Every source on a cone around that axis produces identical inter-microphone delays. In a plane this collapses to a front/back mirror: θ and 180° − θ are indistinguishable.
  • No elevation. A talker standing and the same talker seated at the same bearing are the same signal.
  • No range. Two talkers on one bearing at different distances cannot be separated at all.

Mitigations, in order of cost: mount the array against a wall, table edge or display bezel so the rear hemisphere is physically baffled; use the 16th, rear-facing microphone as a front/back discriminator and noise reference; and if genuine 2-D coverage is needed, this design becomes the row element of an L or cross array — the beamformer mathematics is unchanged, only the steering manifold grows.

§3

Geometry

Base grid pitch is 21.4 mm, which is λ/2 at 8 kHz. That is the binding spatial-sampling constraint: to steer to ±75° without a grating lobe entering visible space the pitch must satisfy d ≤ λ/(1+sinθ) = 42.88/1.966 = 21.8 mm, so 21.4 mm holds with a little margin.

§4

Beamformer

Stack every FIR tap into one vector h. The array’s response to a plane wave is then linear in h, and the whole design collapses to a single regularised least-squares problem:

minimise   Σ_f w_f |a(f)ᵀh − t(f)|²         response fit
         + γ Σ_f u_f · hᵀ Re(Eᴇ Γ(f) E) h   diffuse-field noise power
         + ρ Σ_f u_f · hᵀ Re(Eᴇ E) h        white (self-) noise power

with target t(f) = S(f)·e^(−j2πf·τ(f)) — the intended band shape carrying the intended group delay. Three things make it practical: the normal-equation matrix is block-Toeplitz, so the 2880 × 2880 system assembles from 1-D lag kernels in about a second; γ is the directivity knob and ρ the robustness knob; and — the trap — the noise weight u_f must be non-zero at every frequency, including outside the output band.

Trap, hit while building this

Where u_f is zero the individual channel gains are unconstrained. The fit only asks that they cancel on axis, and the superdirective solution happily makes them enormous and opposite. That costs nothing on paper. In the first version of this design it produced tens of dB of out-of-band self-noise gain and no tolerance to mismatch whatsoever. Holding u_f at 0.25 outside the band fixes it.

Operating point: γ = 0.1, ρ = 0.01, 192 taps per microphone at 48 kHz — a 4.00 ms filter span.

Directivity rises toward endfire — the main lobe narrows in the steering plane and the ambiguity cone shrinks. More usefully, group delay is identical for every beam, so a beam switch produces no timing step, only a gain cross-fade.

Beams are spaced uniformly in sinθ, not in angle, which is what keeps the main lobe a constant width across coverage. At a spacing of 0.06 that is 34 beams over ±75°, and a talker anywhere in coverage is within 1.2 dB of a beam centre. Widening to 21 beams costs 3.2 dB; to 14 beams, 7.7 dB.

Why the group delay is not constant

The single most important latency decision. The target delay tapers from 1.50 ms below 200 Hz to 0.70 ms above 3 kHz, for two independent reasons that happen to agree.

Aperture crossing is a hard physical floor. Any beamformer that coherently sums an aperture A steered at θ must wait for the wavefront to cross it: A·sinθ/c. At ±75° the full 513.6 mm low-frequency aperture needs 1.45 ms — so 1.50 ms is that floor plus margin, not an arbitrary choice. But high frequencies only use the inner 128.4 mm sub-aperture, needing 0.36 ms. Making them wait for the low-frequency aperture would throw away a millisecond for nothing.

The ear tolerates delay unevenly. Blauert & Laws put the audibility threshold for group delay at roughly 1.6 ms at 8 kHz, 2 ms at 4 kHz, 3.2 ms at 1 kHz and 10 ms at 500 Hz. Spending delay where it is both needed and inaudible is free.

The taper has to stay gentle

A steep dτ/df is an all-pass a short FIR cannot realise, and it is paid for in on-axis ripple. An early attempt at 1.6 → 0.35 ms over 250–2000 Hz produced 7.5 dB of ripple. The shipped 1.50 → 0.70 ms over 200–3000 Hz produces 1.00 dB.

Two notes on the numbers. The converters dominate, not the DSP — 0.63 ms of the 1.42 ms is ADC and DAC filtering, and those two figures are allocations, not measurements. They assume the chosen converters offer a short-group-delay filter mode and that it is enabled; a converter left in default linear-phase mode can be three to five times worse and would dominate the budget on its own. And the AGC has no look-ahead: the conventional look-ahead limiter would add 0.3–1 ms, so this design takes soft-knee compression with a fast release and a hard clip guard instead, accepting a few tenths of a dB of transient overshoot rather than paying for it on every sample.

For scale: 1.42 ms is 0.49 m of air. Used for reinforcement in the same room, the array is acoustically nearer the listener than a loudspeaker half a metre away, so the Haas-effect budget is essentially untouched.

§6

Finding the talker

SRP-PHAT runs on a 16 kHz path: 32 ms frames, 16 ms hop, 300–3800 Hz, across a 65-point angular grid with a parabolic fit through the peak for sub-grid resolution. It sits off the audio path — its 32 ms of analysis latency costs nothing because it only selects which pre-computed beam is active. The audio never passes through it.

Rather than summing all 105 microphone-pair cross-spectra per direction, the identity Σₖ<ₖₖ Re(xᵢxₖ* e^−ʲᵧΔτ) = (|Σᵢ xᵢdᵢ*|² − M)/2 gives identical values in O(M) instead of O(M²).

The median is excellent throughout. The interesting column is the right-hand one: with a competing talker only 3 dB down, more than a quarter of frames point at the wrong person. Per-frame localisation is not talker tracking.

The tracker

Which is why the raw peak never steers anything. Between SRP-PHAT and the beam bank sits a hysteretic state machine:

  • Per-beam scores smoothed with a 120 ms time constant.
  • A per-beam noise floor tracked as a 5 s running minimum and subtracted — this is what stops HVAC, a projector fan or a laptop on the table from becoming “the talker”.
  • A challenger must beat the held beam by 3 dB continuously for 150 ms before the beam moves.
  • 400 ms hangover after speech offset, so the beam does not wander during natural pauses.
  • Beam changes cross-fade over 30 ms between the two running beamformers.

Measured on a turn-taking scenario — talker A at −35° for three seconds, then talker B at +40°: the beam is correctly on A for 100 % of frames before hand-over, locks onto B in 160 ms, and switches once in six seconds. One switch, where one switch was warranted. The 160 ms is essentially the 150 ms hysteresis window — the tracker is as fast as it is allowed to be, and the trade is explicit and tunable rather than emergent.

§7

Two different gains

The classic error in superdirective array design is to quote the directivity index and imply it applies to all noise. It does not.

  • Room noise is close to a diffuse, spherically isotropic field. The array’s gain against it is the directivity index.
  • Microphone self-noise is uncorrelated between elements. The array’s gain against it is the white-noise gain — which a superdirective design drives negative at low frequency.

A-weighted over the 120 Hz–8 kHz output band, referenced to a single omni microphone through the same band shaping:

This designDelay-and-sum
Gain against diffuse room noise+6.1 dB+3.9 dB
Effect on microphone self-noise−10.2 dB−11.8 dB

Superdirectivity buys 2.2 dB more room-noise rejection and gives back 1.6 dB of self-noise benefit. Since microphone self-noise is 29 dBA and a real room is 40–45 dBA, room noise dominates and the trade is clearly worth taking — but it is a trade, and ρ is the knob if a quieter room or a noisier microphone shifts the balance.

The beamformer dominates, and it is dominated in turn by the 192-tap length — set by the 4 ms filter span needed to shape a beam at 200 Hz. That is the real cost of low-frequency directivity, and it is paid in MACs rather than in latency only because of the group-delay taper.

One rigid board, four distributed converters

The most important build decision, and it follows straight from the tolerance budget. Element position tolerance is ±0.3 mm. On a single board, position is set by fab artwork and pick-and-place — roughly ±0.05 mm each, ±0.07 mm combined, comfortably inside budget. Split the array across two boards joined by a connector or flex tail and position becomes an assembly tolerance instead, stacking up mechanical play that lands directly on the parameter the beamformer is most sensitive to. A 545 mm board is awkward to panel and costs more per unit area. Pay it.

The outermost microphone sits 256.8 mm from centre, so routing it to a centrally placed converter would mean a quarter-metre single-ended analogue run past a TDM bus. Four 4-channel ADCs placed near their own microphone groups keep the longest analogue run under about 90 mm; they share one TDM bus and one MCLK, so it costs pins nowhere. Stack, top to bottom: signal / unbroken ground / split power / digital and TDM. The ground plane under the analogue section must not be cut.

What actually sets the noise, and what sets the matching

The microphone’s own output noise density is about 60 nV/√Hz — 29 dBA of self-noise at −38 dBV/Pa is 7.1 µV RMS A-weighted over roughly 13 kHz of noise bandwidth. That dominates completely: a 10 nV/√Hz op-amp adds 0.11 dB to the channel, a 20 nV/√Hz part 0.43 dB. The preamp is chosen for supply current, DC behaviour and channel matching, not for noise, which frees the choice considerably. With 0.1 % gain resistors the stage contributes about 0.012 dB of channel-to-channel gain error.

Why the calibration FIR is not optional

MEMS sensitivity tolerance is ±1 dB for a well-specified part and ±3 dB for a cheap one — two to six times the 0.5 dB the tolerance Monte-Carlo assumes. Calibration is not trimming a second-order effect; it is what brings the array inside the tolerance its own performance figures were computed at.

Power

Microphone PSRR is around −60 dB, so supply ripple lands more or less straight in the audio: the analogue rail gets its own low-noise LDO, and the switchers stay on the far side of the split. Budget ±12 V at 100 mA for the line driver — enough for a 600 Ω load at 10.3 mA plus quiescent current and margin — and post-filter it with LC plus an LDO, because a DC-DC switching at 100–500 kHz is out of band but its harmonics will intermodulate if let through.

Mechanical

Bottom-port microphones on the top side over a PCB through-hole, so the acoustic reference plane is the board face. Use the vendor’s recommended port diameter for a 1.6 mm board and confirm the port tube resonance sits above 20 kHz. Sealed ports with gaskets, mesh for dust and pop, and the PCB decoupled from the enclosure on compliant mounts — a structure-borne vibration path is coherent across every element, so it beamforms perfectly and arrives at full array gain. Vibration is the one noise source the array actively makes worse.

Bring-up order

Each stage ends in a measurement, so a fault is localised when it appears rather than at the end.

  • Bare board — continuity and isolation on all rails.
  • Power only — every rail’s voltage, and the analogue rail’s ripple under 10 µV RMS in band, measured rather than assumed.
  • DSP and clock — MCLK frequency and jitter, before anything downstream depends on them.
  • ADCs, microphones not yet fitted — all 16 TDM slots should read the converter noise floor. Confirm the four devices leave reset synchronously: they share MCLK and LRCLK, so sample instants are simultaneous even though the bus is serial. Verify that rather than assuming it — it is a common source of unexplained beamformer degradation when it is not true.
  • Microphones — per-channel sensitivity and noise floor in the coupler fixture.
  • DAC and line driver — a −14 dBFS test tone should give +4 dBu at the XLR; check the clip point, and check for asymmetric clipping, which points at a rail or bias problem.
  • Calibrate, then acceptance test.

Calibration — and a trap

You cannot calibrate this array far-field in a room

The Fraunhofer distance for a 513.6 mm aperture is 2D²/λ: 1.54 m at 1 kHz, 6.15 m at 4 kHz, and 12.30 m at 8 kHz. A reference loudspeaker at the far end of a large anechoic chamber is still in the near field at the top of the band, and calibrating against a wavefront you have wrongly assumed to be plane writes that error permanently into the correction filters.

So calibrate per element, not per array. Each microphone is measured in a small pressure coupler — a sealed cavity small compared to a wavelength, in which every element sees identical pressure by construction, with no far-field requirement at all. A 10 mm cavity puts its first mode at 17.1 kHz, comfortably above the band edge; 6 mm gives 28.6 kHz. Measure gain and phase against a reference microphone, fit the 7-tap correction FIR, store it in NVM. Whole-array measurement then becomes an acceptance test rather than a calibration source — and one that only has to be trusted to a couple of dB, which a treated room at 2 m can deliver.

You cannot hand-build a prototype

MEMS microphones are damaged by hand soldering, cannot be ultrasonically cleaned, and need controlled reflow with MSL handling. Even a one-off needs a stencil and a proper profile. Allow 4–6 weeks from ordering parts to a powered board, with the lead-time swing on the DSP and the isolated DC-DC.

Two things that will bite the schedule

Confirm the converter’s low-latency filter mode at datasheet stage, before layout. It is 0.63 ms of the 1.42 ms budget. If the chosen part does not offer one the headline specification fails, and finding out after layout costs a re-spin.

The 545 mm board may not fit the assembler’s machine. A number of common placement machines top out around 508 mm; long-rail machines reach 610 mm and beyond, but not every contract manufacturer has one. Confirm before layout, because the obvious fix — splitting the board — is exactly what the tolerance argument forbids, and would put element position back into assembly tolerance.

If no long-rail assembler is available, the cheap escape is to pull the outer element pair in from ±12 to ±11 grid units:

§11

Proof of concept

The production build is not the right first thing to make. Two of the design’s risks are independent, and each retires far more cheaply alone than together.

  • Does the beamformer and tracker behave in a real room? Needs 15 sample-synchronous channels at known positions. Needs no low latency at all, because it can be validated on recordings, offline.
  • Can the chain hit 1.42 ms? Needs converter measurements. Needs no array.

Neither needs a custom board. Splitting them is what makes a proof of concept a few hundred dollars and a couple of weekends instead of a board spin.

What cannot go on a breadboard

The analogue front end is fine on one — audio-band signals at low impedance forgive contact resistance and a few pF of stray. Two things are not.

The microphones. MEMS parts are destroyed by hand soldering. Use electret capsules instead: through-hole, hand-solderable, a dollar or two, and at roughly −38 dBV/Pa with 58–65 dB SNR they are within a few dB of the MEMS the production design assumes. A 6–10 mm capsule also fits comfortably inside the 21.4 mm grid pitch.

The digital side. MCLK at 24.576 MHz and TDM over solderless jumpers, with no ground plane, is where a breadboard actually fails. Do not breadboard the converters — buy the capture instead. A multichannel USB audio interface samples all its channels simultaneously, which is the only property the array requires; its own latency is irrelevant when the validation is offline.

The geometry does not go on the breadboard either

Element positions are set by a drilled strip — aluminium, acrylic, even MDF — with capsules in the holes and flying leads back to protoboard. The breadboard carries bias resistors and coupling caps, nothing positional.

Two measurements make this easier than it sounds. The array is insensitive to position: ±1 mm costs 0.03 dB of directivity, so a drill press and a printed template suffice. And more useful still, error you measure is not error at all — design the filters for the geometry you actually built rather than the one you drew:

Position 1σDesigned for nominalDesigned for measured
0.5 mm12.06 dB12.07 dB
2.0 mm11.97 dB12.06 dB
4.0 mm11.69 dB12.03 dB

You have one unit, so per-unit geometry calibration is free. Measure the holes with calipers, put the numbers in, and the mechanical tolerance problem disappears. Element gain and phase still need calibrating — that is where the real sensitivity lives, worth about 1.5 dB of directivity.

Start below the full design. An eight-element uniform array at 42.8 mm pitch needs one eight-channel interface and captures most of the behaviour:

What it does not prove is the low-frequency superdirectivity, which needs the full 513.6 mm aperture. Roughly $350: eight electrets, a drilled strip, passives, and an eight-channel interface with microphone preamps.

Stage two: sixteen channels, then latency

Extend the strip to the full fifteen positions and add a second interface, clock-linked to the first over ADAT or word clock so the channels stay sample-synchronous. Around $600 all in. This validates the design as drawn: measured directivity, beam patterns and tracker behaviour against the predictions on this page.

Latency is a separate, later experiment that does not involve the array at all. Take the candidate ADC and DAC evaluation boards, enable the low-latency filter mode, and measure the group delay of each. That is the 0.63 ms flagged as an allocation in the latency budget, it is the single largest uncertainty in the headline specification, and two evaluation boards retire it.

What the proof of concept confirms is the acoustics and the algorithms in a real room with real reverberation and real talkers — everything the simulation assumed and could not itself check. It says nothing about latency, production self-noise, manufacturability or cost; those are retired by different experiments.

Schematics

Section-level, not board-level: enough to review the topology and the values the analysis actually determines, with the parts that still need detailed design left visible as blocks rather than pretended into existence.

Deliberately out of scope

  • Acoustic echo cancellation, needed for duplex conferencing. A time-domain NLMS AEC adds no latency and would sit between the beamformer and the AGC, but it is a substantial subsystem in its own right.
  • Dereverberation. The array attenuates the reverberant field by construction; explicit dereverberation adds latency and is rarely worth it at this DI.
  • Multiple simultaneous outputs. The architecture extends naturally — 138 MMAC/s per additional talker feed — but “the primary talker” was specified as singular.
